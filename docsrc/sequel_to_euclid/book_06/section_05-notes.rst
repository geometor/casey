Book VI, Section V Notes: Poncelet's Porism, Hart's Proof & Apollonius
======================================================================

.. folio:: 0139

1. Historical Provenance: Poncelet, Jacobi, Hart & Casey
--------------------------------------------------------

Section V brings together several remarkable chapters in nineteenth-century geometry:

* **Louis Gaultier (1813):** In *Journal de l'École Polytechnique* 16, Gaultier introduced the concepts of *axe radical* and *centre radical*, providing synthetic constructions for non-intersecting circles.
* **Jean-Victor Poncelet (1822):** In his foundational *Traité des propriétés projectives des figures*, Poncelet developed the theory of coaxal systems and discovered the limiting points (*points limites*). He also formulated **Poncelet's Porism (Poncelet's Closure Theorem)**: if a polygon inscribed in one conic/circle and circumscribed about another closes after $n$ steps for one starting point, it closes for *every* starting point!
* **Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi (1828):** Jacobi gave the first rigorous analytic proof of Poncelet's Porism using elliptic functions, showing that each step of the polygonal chain corresponds to a constant addition $u \mapsto u + \tau$ on an elliptic curve.
* **Andrew Searle Hart (1857):** Hart discovered the elegant elementary synthetic proof presented in Prop. 12 (pp. 125–126), circumventing elliptic integrals entirely through properties of coaxal pencils and limiting points.
* **John Casey (1858):** Casey independently investigated coaxal circles and Poncelet's porism, presenting papers to the Royal Irish Academy and Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society.

2. Apollonius of Perga & Sir Isaac Newton
-----------------------------------------

* **Problem of Apollonius (Prop. 10):** The classical problem of constructing a circle tangent to three given circles was solved in Apollonius' lost treatise *Tangencies* ($\Pi\epsilon\rho\grave{\iota}\ \epsilon\pi\alpha\phi\tilde{\omega}\nu$). Viète (1600) reconstructed it using Euclidean tools, and Gergonne (1816) solved it via poles and polars. Casey presents both the Dublin inversion method and Newton's method from the *Principia* (Book I, Lemma XVI).
