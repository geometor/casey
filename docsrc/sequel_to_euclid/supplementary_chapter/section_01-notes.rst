1. Isogonal Transformation & Pedal Circles
------------------------------------------

Section I opens Casey's systematic codification of triangle conjugation. While Steiner and Catalan noted special cases of pedal concurrency earlier in the nineteenth century, the general synthetic theory of isogonal conjugates and their six-point common pedal circle forms the foundation of the 1873–1886 Franco-Belgian geometry movement.

2. From Lhuilier & Grebe to Lemoine (1873) and d'Ocagne's "Symmedian"
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Although the minimum-square property of $K = X(6)$ had been noticed in passing by Simon Lhuilier (1809) and Ernst Wilhelm Grebe (1847—hence the German name *Grebescher Punkt*), modern triangle geometry began in earnest at the **1873 Lyon Congress** of the *Association Française pour l'Avancement des Sciences*, where :person:`Émile Lemoine <lemoine>` presented :cite:`*Note sur un point remarquable du plan d'un triangle* <lemoine:1873>`. Ten years later, Maurice d'Ocagne (*Nouvelles Annales de Mathématiques*, 1883) coined the enduring French term *symédiane* ("symmedian").

When :person:`John Casey <casey>` added his *Supplementary Chapter* to the 4th edition of the *Sequel* in 1886 (expanded further in the 1888 5th and 1892 6th editions), it became the **first textbook in any language** to unify Lemoine, Brocard, Tucker, Taylor, and Neuberg into Euclidean propositions. :person:`Nathan Altshiller-Court's <altshiller-court>` Chapter X (*Recent Geometry of the Triangle*, §§ 557–590 in ``geometor/altshiller-court``) descends directly from Casey's Section I architecture, and ``geometor/etc`` indexes Lemoine's point as ``X(6)``.
