Book III: Additional Propositions & Exercises¶

Additional Propositions & Exercises Extending Euclid’s Book III (pp. 29–52, Folios 0055–0078)

Book III of John Casey’s Sequel to Euclid (5th ed., 1888) establishes the modern theory of the circle: inverse points, circle power, orthogonal circles, the radical axis, the radical centre, Poncelet’s limiting points, and the foundational projective theory of poles and polars (La Hire).

Outline of Sections¶

I. Additional Propositions (Props. 1–29)¶

Book III, Section I: Additional Propositions (1888 English)

Prop. 22 (:term:`Orthogonal Circles <orthogonal-circles>`). Two circles $(O_1, r_1)$ and $(O_2, r_2)$ cut each other orthogonally (so that the tangents at their points of intersection are at right angles) if and only if the square of the distance between their centres equals the sum of the squares of their radii:

$$ |O_1 O_2|^2 = r_1^2 + r_2^2. $$

Equivalently, any secant drawn from the centre of one orthogonal circle through the other cuts it in two points which are inverse with respect to the first circle.

Book III, Section I Notes — Historical & Cross-Canon Commentary

The concept of the Radical Axis (axe radical) and Radical Centre (centre radical) was introduced by Louis Gaultier of Tours in his 1813 memoir Les contacts des cercles (Journal de l’École Polytechnique 9, cahier 16, pp. 124–214). Gaultier showed that every pair of non-concentric circles possesses a straight-line locus of equal tangent lengths, which serves as the natural coordinate axis for coaxial pencils.

Book III — Section II: Summary

Torricelli / Fermat Point & Napoleon Triangle Configurations (Ex. 6–9): - Construction of equilateral triangles on the three sides of an arbitrary triangle leads to concurrent circumcircles at the first isogonic center $X(13)$ (Fermat-Torricelli point). - Lines connecting original vertices to opposite external vertices are concurrent and equal in length. - The circumcentres form an equilateral triangle (Outer Napoleon Triangle); reversing orientation yields the Inner Napoleon Triangle, with sum of squares relation $\Sigma s_{\text{nap}}^2 = \Sigma s_{\text{orig}}^2$.

English · Summary · Notes

Book III — Section II: Notes

Exercises 6–9 anticipate major 19th-century triangle discoveries: - Exercise 6 & 7: Pierre de Fermat proposed to Torricelli the problem of finding a point $P$ minimizing the sum of distances to three vertices: $PA + PB + PC$. Torricelli solved it using equilateral triangles described on the sides, demonstrating concurrence of circles and connecting segments at $\angle APB = \angle BPC = \angle CPA = 120^\circ$. - Exercise 8 & 9: The theorem attributing the equilateral triangle of circumcentres to Napoleon Bonaparte appeared in print in the early 19th century (e.g. Rutherford in 1825). Casey provides a direct synthetic framework for the theorem and its dual inwards-oriented counterpart.
