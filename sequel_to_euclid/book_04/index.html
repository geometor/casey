Book IV: Additional Propositions & Exercises¶

Additional Propositions & Exercises Extending Euclid’s Book IV (pp. 53–66, Folios 0079–0092)

Book IV of John Casey’s Sequel to Euclid (5th ed., 1888) treats inscribed, circumscribed, and escribed circles of triangles and regular polygons. It establishes fundamental relations between inradii and exradii, Heron’s formula for triangle area, metric properties of regular polygons, the Nine-Points Circle (Euler, Feuerbach), the tangency of the Nine-Points Circle to the incircle and excircles (Feuerbach’s Theorem), circumcircle bisector projections, and mean centres of vertices.

I. Additional Propositions (Props. 1–9)¶

Book IV, Section I: Additional Propositions (Props. 1–9)

Prop. 1.—If a circle be inscribed in a triangle, the distances from the angular points of the triangle to the points of contact on the sides are respectively equal to the remainders that are left, when the lengths of the sides are taken separately from their half sum.

Book IV, Section I Notes — Historical & Mathematical Commentary

Although Leonhard Euler (1765) showed that the circumcentre, centroid, and orthocentre are collinear and that the circle through the altitude feet passes through the side midpoints, the explicit concyclicity of the full nine points was first published jointly by Charles Julien Brianchon and Jean-Victor Poncelet in Gergonne’s Annales de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées 11 (1820–1821).

Book IV, Section II Notes — Historical Commentary

Exercise 31 highlights the rich hierarchy of circles associated with a triangle: the four points $I, I_a, I_b, I_c$ form an orthocentric system where each point is the orthocentre of the triangle formed by the other three. The circumcircle of $\triangle ABC$ is precisely the Nine-Points Circle of each of these four triangles. Consequently, by Feuerbach’s theorem, the circumcircle of $\triangle ABC$ touches all sixteen inscribed and escribed circles of the four triangles!
