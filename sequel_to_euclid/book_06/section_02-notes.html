Book VI, Section II Notes: Monge’s Theorem & The Circle of Similitude¶

Historical Context & Cross-Canon Lineage

f. 0108 · p. 82

1. Monge’s Theorem (1798)¶

Gaspard Monge discovered the theorem of the three collinear centres of similitude in 1798 using a brilliant 3D projection argument:

  • Regard three coplanar circles as the equatorial sections of three spheres of the same radii in 3-space.

  • Enclose the three spheres in a common tangent cone or plane.

  • The vertices of the circumscribed tangent cones are the centres of similitude of the equatorial circles, and the apexes must lie in the plane tangent to all three spheres—intersecting the plane of circles along a straight line!

Casey’s proof in Section II, Prop. 3 (pp. 84–85) is purely 2-dimensional and synthetic, deriving Monge’s theorem directly from Menelaus’s transversal theorem applied to the triangle of centres $\triangle ABC$.

2. Circle of Similitude & Apollonius¶

The Circle of Similitude (Prop. 5) directly realizes Apollonius's circle locus for the ratio $r_1 : r_2$. Because $S_e$ and $S_i$ divide the line of centres internally and externally in the same ratio, segment $S_e S_i$ forms a harmonic diameter with respect to the two centres $O_1, O_2$.

3. Concordance with Altshiller-Court¶

This section corresponds to Nathan Altshiller-Court’s College Geometry (1952), Chapter II (Similitude and Homothecy, §§ 40–53): * § 40: Centres of similitude; * § 44: Homologous points on circles; * § 49: Monge’s Theorem on collinearity of similitude centres; * § 53: Circle of similitude as the locus of equal angular subtense.
