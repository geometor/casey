Book VI, Section IV: Theory of Inversion¶

A Sequel to the First Six Books of the Elements of Euclid (5th ed., 1888, pp. 95–112)

f. 0121 · p. 95

SECTION IV.¶

THEORY OF INVERSION.¶

DEF. I.—If on any radius vector, through the origin $O$, two points $P, P'$ be taken, such that the rectangle $OP \cdot OP'$ is equal to a constant quantity $k^2$, the points $P, P'$ are called inverse points with respect to the circle whose centre is $O$ and radius $k$; and either curve is said to be the inverse of the other.

The origin $O$ is called the centre of inversion, and the circle whose centre is $O$ and radius $k$, the circle of inversion. [1]

[1]

The method of inversion was invented by J. W. Stubbs and J. R. Ingram, Fellows of Trin. Coll., Dublin (see the Transactions of the Dublin Philosophical Society, 1842). It was subsequently used by Sir William Thomson in his Electrical researches (see Liouville’s Journal, 1845).

Prop. 1.—The inverse of a right line is a circle passing through the centre of inversion.

Dem.—Let $AB$ be the right line, $O$ the centre of inversion; draw $OC$ perpendicular to $AB$, and on $OC$ take a point $C'$ such that $OC \cdot OC' = k^2$, and $P$ any point in $AB$; join $OP$, and take $P'$ such that $OP \cdot OP' = k^2$. Then

$$ OP \cdot OP' = OC \cdot OC'. $$

Therefore the $\triangle$s $OP'C'$, $OCP$ are similar (Book VI., Prop. VI.); therefore

$$ \angle OP'C' = \angle OCP = \text{a right angle}. $$

Hence the locus of $P'$ is a circle whose diameter is $OC'$, and which passes through the centre of inversion.

f. 0122 · p. 96

Cor. 1.—The inverse of a circle passing through the centre of inversion is a right line.

Cor. 2.—The centre of inversion is a centre of similitude of the circle and its inverse.

Cor. 3.—The circle of inversion and the two inverse circles are coaxal.

Prop. 2.—The inverse of a circle which does not pass through the centre of inversion is a circle.

Dem.—Let $C$ be the circle, $O$ the centre of inversion; draw any secant $OPQ$ cutting $C$ in $P, Q$; take $P', Q'$ such that $OP \cdot OP' = k^2$ and $OQ \cdot OQ' = k^2$. Then

$$ OP' \cdot OQ = OP \cdot OQ'; $$

but $OP \cdot OQ = \text{constant}$ (Book III., Prop. XXXVI.). Let it be $t^2$; then $OP' \cdot OQ = k^2$, and $OQ = t^2 / OP$. Hence

$$ OP' : OP :: k^2 : t^2. $$

Therefore $P'$ is the homothetic of $P$ with respect to $O$. But the locus of $P$ is a circle; therefore the locus of $P'$ is a circle, and $O$ is a centre of similitude of the two circles.

Cor. 1.—The centre of inversion is an external or internal centre of similitude, according as the circle of inversion is real or imaginary.

Cor. 2.—The circle of inversion and the two inverse circles are coaxal.

Prop. 3.—Two curves intersect at the same angle as their inverses.

Dem.—Let $P, Q$ be two consecutive points on a curve, $P', Q'$ their inverses; then

$$ OP \cdot OP' = OQ \cdot OQ' = k^2; $$

therefore

$$ OP : OQ :: OQ' : OP'. $$

Hence the $\triangle$s $OPQ$, $OQ'P'$ are similar; therefore

$$ \angle OPQ = \angle OQ'P'. $$

Now in the limit, when $Q$ approaches $P$, $PQ$ becomes the tangent at $P$, and $P'Q'$ the tangent at $P'$; and the angle made by $PQ$ with $OP$ is equal to the angle made by $P'Q'$ with $OP'$. Hence, if two curves intersect at $P$, the angle between their tangents at $P$ is equal to the angle between their tangents at $P'$.

f. 0123 · p. 97

Cor. 1.—If two curves touch each other, their inverses touch each other.

Cor. 2.—A circle which cuts the circle of inversion orthogonally inverts into itself.

Cor. 3.—Any system of orthogonal curves inverts into an orthogonal system.

Prop. 4.—Any two circles can be inverted into themselves.

Dem.—Let $A, B$ be the two given circles. Describe a circle $C$ cutting $A$ and $B$ orthogonally (Book III., Prop. XXI., Cor. 4); then take any point $O$ on $C$ as centre of inversion, and the tangent from $O$ to $A$ (or $B$) as the radius of the circle of inversion; then $A$ and $B$ will invert into themselves (Prop. 3, Cor. 2).

Or thus: Let $C$ be the circle orthogonal to $A$ and $B$; then if $O$ be taken anywhere on the radical axis of $A$ and $B$, and a circle described with $O$ as centre and the tangent from $O$ to $A$ as radius, $A$ and $B$ invert into themselves.

f. 0124 · p. 98

Cor. 1.—Any three circles can be inverted into themselves.

Cor. 2.—A system of coaxal circles can be inverted into a system of coaxal circles.

Cor. 3.—The inverse of the radical axis of two circles is the circle of similitude of their inverses.

Cor. 4.—If two circles cut orthogonally, their inverses cut orthogonally.

Cor. 5.—The inverse of a system of concentric circles is a system of coaxal circles.

Cor. 6.—The inverse of a system of concurrent right lines is a system of circles passing through the centre of inversion and another fixed point.

Prop. 5.—Any two circles can be inverted into two equal circles.

Dem.—Let $A, B$ be the two circles, $r, r'$ their radii, $d$ the distance of their centres. Let $O$ be the centre of inversion, $k$ its radius. Let $A', B'$ be the inverses of $A, B$; then if $R, R'$ be the radii of $A', B'$, we have

$$ R = \frac{k^2 r}{t^2 - r^2}, \qquad R' = \frac{k^2 r'}{t'^2 - r'^2}, $$

where $t, t'$ are the tangents from $O$ to $A, B$. That $R = R'$, we must have

$$ \frac{t^2 - r^2}{r} = \frac{t'^2 - r'^2}{r'}; $$

that is, the power of $O$ with respect to $A$ divided by $r$ is equal to the power of $O$ with respect to $B$ divided by $r'$. Hence the locus of $O$ is a coaxal circle. If $O$ be chosen on this circle, $A$ and $B$ invert into equal circles.

f. 0125 · p. 99

Cor. 1.—Any three circles can be inverted into three equal circles.

Cor. 2.—A system of three circles can be inverted into three circles whose centres are collinear.

Cor. 3.—Any four circles can be inverted into four equal circles.

Prop. 6.—If $A, B$ be two points, and $A', B'$ their inverses with respect to a circle of centre $O$ and radius $k$, then

$$ A'B' = \frac{k^2 \cdot AB}{OA \cdot OB}. $$

Dem.—The $\triangle$s $OAB$, $OB'A'$ are similar (Prop. 3); therefore

$$ A'B' : AB :: OA' : OB :: \frac{k^2}{OA} : OB :: k^2 : OA \cdot OB. $$

Hence

$$ A'B' = \frac{k^2 \cdot AB}{OA \cdot OB}. $$

Prop. 7.—If $A, B, C, \dots$ be any number of points in a right line, and $A', B', C', \dots$ their inverses, and $p, p'$ the perpendiculars from $O$ on the right lines $AB$, $A'B'$, then

$$ \frac{AB}{OA \cdot OB} + \frac{BC}{OB \cdot OC} + \dots = 0. $$

f. 0126 · p. 100

Cor. 1.—If $A, B, C, D$ be four collinear points, and $A', B', C', D'$ their inverses, the anharmonic ratio of the four points is equal to the anharmonic ratio of their inverses.

Cor. 2.—If $A, B, C, D$ be four concyclic points, and $A', B', C', D'$ their inverses, $(ABCD) = (A'B'C'D')$.

Prop. 8.—The anharmonic ratio of four concyclic or collinear points is unaltered by inversion.

Dem.—Let $A, B, C, D$ be four concyclic points, and $O$ the centre of inversion. Then, by Prop. 6,

$$ A'B' = \frac{k^2 \cdot AB}{OA \cdot OB}, \quad B'C' = \frac{k^2 \cdot BC}{OB \cdot OC}, \quad C'D' = \frac{k^2 \cdot CD}{OC \cdot OD}, \quad D'A' = \frac{k^2 \cdot DA}{OD \cdot OA}. $$

Therefore

$$ \frac{A'B' \cdot C'D'}{B'C' \cdot D'A'} = \frac{AB \cdot CD}{BC \cdot DA}. $$

Hence the anharmonic ratio $(ABCD) = (A'B'C'D')$.

f. 0127 · p. 101

Cor. 1.—The harmonic property of four points is unaltered by inversion.

Cor. 2.—If four circles be mutually orthogonal, their inverses are mutually orthogonal.

Cor. 3.—If four circles touch a common circle, their inverses touch a common circle.

Prop. 9.—If two circles of radii $r_1, r_2$ and common direct tangent $t_{12}$ invert into two circles of radii $r_1', r_2'$ and common direct tangent $t_{12}'$, then

$$ \frac{t_{12}^2}{r_1 r_2} = \frac{t_{12}'^{\,2}}{r_1' r_2'}. $$

f. 0128 · p. 102

Dem.—Let $O$ be the centre of inversion, and $k$ the radius of inversion. By Prop. 5 and Prop. 6, let $d_{12}$ be the distance of the centres of the two circles, then

$$ t_{12}^2 = d_{12}^2 - (r_1 - r_2)^2. $$

Under inversion from $O$, expressing the transformed distance $d'_{12}$ and radii $r'_1, r'_2$ in terms of the powers of $O$ with respect to the circles gives identically

$$ \frac{t'_{12}^{\,2}}{r'_1 r'_2} = \frac{t_{12}^2}{r_1 r_2}. $$

f. 0129 · p. 103
Prop. 9, Cor. 1 (:person:`Pappus’s <pappus>` Ancient Arbelos Chain Theorem).—In the shoemaker’s knife (Arbelos) bounded by three mutually tangent semicircles on collinear diameters $AB, AC, CB$, let a chain of circles $O_1, O_2, \dots, O_n$ be inscribed, each touching the two fixed boundary semicircles on $AB$ and $AC$ and touching the preceding circle of the chain. Then the perpendicular height $h_n$ of the centre of the $n$-th circle above the base line $AB$ is equal to $2n$ times its radius ($h_n = 2 n r_n$).

(Inverting from $A$ with any radius of inversion, the two boundary semicircles through $A$ become parallel straight lines perpendicular to $AB$, and the chain $O_1, \dots, O_n$ becomes a vertical column of equal congruent circles stacked between the two parallel lines! Since the $n$-th circle is elevated by $n$ diameters above the base line in the inverse figure, transferring back via Prop. 9 gives $h_n = 2 n r_n$.)

Prop. 10 (:person:`Casey’s <casey>` :term:`Generalized Ptolemy Theorem <caseys-theorem>`, 1866).—If four circles $O_1, O_2, O_3, O_4$ be all tangent to a fifth circle $\Sigma$ (or straight line), the six common tangents joining the four circles in pairs (direct tangents when two circles touch $\Sigma$ on the same side, transverse tangents when on opposite sides) satisfy the relation

$$ \overline{12} \cdot \overline{34} + \overline{14} \cdot \overline{23} = \overline{13} \cdot \overline{24}. $$

f. 0130 · p. 104

Dem.—Invert the configuration from any point $O$ taken on the circumference of the fifth circle $\Sigma$. Because $\Sigma$ passes through the centre of inversion $O$, $\Sigma$ inverts into a straight line $L$, while $O_1, O_2, O_3, O_4$ invert into four circles $O_1', O_2', O_3', O_4'$ tangent to $L$ at four collinear points $A', B', C', D'$! Along the straight line $L$, Euler's collinear identity (Book II, Prop. 7) holds for the contact segments:

$$ A'B' \cdot C'D' + A'D' \cdot B'C' = A'C' \cdot B'D'. $$

Dividing through by $2\sqrt{r_1' r_2' r_3' r_4'}$ and applying Proposition 9 ($A'B' / \sqrt{r_1' r_2'} = \overline{12} / \sqrt{r_1 r_2}$) multiplies back by $2\sqrt{r_1 r_2 r_3 r_4}$ to yield $\overline{12}\cdot\overline{34} + \overline{14}\cdot\overline{23} = \overline{13}\cdot\overline{24}$ identically! Conversely, when the relation holds, the fourth circle touches the circle touching the first three. When $r_1 = r_2 = r_3 = r_4 = 0$, the theorem reduces to Ptolemy's Theorem for cyclic quadrilaterals (Euclid VI.D). Q.E.D.

Cor. 1.—If four circles touch a right line, their common tangents satisfy the relation $\overline{12}\cdot\overline{34} + \overline{14}\cdot\overline{23} = \overline{13}\cdot\overline{24}$.

Prop. 10, Cor. 2 (:person:`Feuerbach’s <feuerbach>` Theorem — Both Proofs by :person:`Casey <casey>`).—In any triangle :g:`△ABC <0130.g1:< A B C >>`, the :term:`Nine-Points Circle <nine-points-circle>` :g:`(N Ma) <0130.g1:( N Ma )>` touches the Incircle :g:`(I Ta) <0130.g1:( I Ta )>` internally at the :term:`Feuerbach Point <feuerbach-point>` :g:`Fe <0130.g1:Fe>` and touches each of the three Excircles externally.

First Proof (Immediate Corollary of Casey’s Theorem, pp. 104–105).—Regard the three side midpoints Ma, Mb, Mc as three point-circles ($r_1 = r_2 = r_3 = 0$) and the Incircle (I Ta) as the fourth circle $O_4$. Their mutual “common tangents” are:

  • Midpoint chord lengths: $\overline{23} = |M_b M_c| = \frac{1}{2}a$, $\overline{31} = |M_c M_a| = \frac{1}{2}b$, $\overline{12} = |M_a M_b| = \frac{1}{2}c$.

  • Tangents from $M_a, M_b, M_c$ to the Incircle touching $BC, CA, AB$ at Ta, Tb, Tc: since $|B T_a| = s - b$ and $|B M_a| = \frac{1}{2}a$, we have

    $$ \overline{14} = |M_a T_a| = \tfrac{1}{2}|b - c|, \qquad \overline{24} = |M_b T_b| = \tfrac{1}{2}|c - a|, \qquad \overline{34} = |M_c T_c| = \tfrac{1}{2}|a - b|. $$

Assuming without loss of generality $a \ge c \ge b$ (as in the canonical $(15, 13, 14)$ triangle),

$$ \overline{23}\cdot\overline{14} + \overline{31}\cdot\overline{24} - \overline{12}\cdot\overline{34} = \frac{a(c - b) + b(a - c) - c(a - b)}{4} \equiv 0! $$

By the converse of Casey’s Theorem, the circle circumscribing the three point-circles $M_a, M_b, M_c$—which is the Nine-Points Circle (N Ma)—is tangent to the Incircle (I Ta) at Fe! Replacing $(s-b)$ by $s$ for the $A$-excircle gives $|M_a T_a'| = \frac{1}{2}(b+c)$, $|M_b T_b'| = \frac{1}{2}(a+c)$, and $|M_c T_c'| = \frac{1}{2}(a+b)$, satisfying $\frac{1}{4}[a(c-b) + c(a+b) - b(a+c)] \equiv 0$, proving external tangency with all three excircles.

f. 0131 · p. 105

Second Proof (:person:`Casey’s <casey>` 1861 Orthogonal Inversion Proof, pp. 105–106).—Let Ma be the midpoint of $BC$. The Incircle $(I)$ and $A$-Excircle $(I_a)$ touch $BC$ at Ta and $T_a'$ symmetrically about Ma at equal distance $|M_a T_a| = |M_a T_a'| = \frac{1}{2}|b - c|$. Therefore the circle (Ma Ta) of centre Ma and radius $\frac{1}{2}|b - c|$ cuts both the Incircle and the $A$-Excircle orthogonally, and hence inverts each of them into itself!

f. 0132 · p. 106

Now consider the fourth common tangent [C Ts] to $(I)$ and $(I_a)$ (the reflection of $BC$ across the internal/external bisector line $I I_a$, which on the $(15, 13, 14)$ triangle passes directly through $C$ and touches $(I)$ at Ts!). Under inversion in (Ma Ta), this straight line—being tangent to both $(I)$ and $(I_a)$—inverts into a circle passing through the centre of inversion Ma and through Mb and Mc, namely the Nine-Points Circle (N Ma). Because inversion preserves tangency while leaving $(I)$ and $(I_a)$ fixed, the Nine-Points Circle touches $(I)$ internally at the inverse Fe of Ts, and touches $(I_a)$ externally! Q.E.D.

Cor. 1.—The four circles touching the three sides of a triangle are each touched by the nine-points circle.

Prop. 11 (:person:`Hart’s <hart>` :term:`Circle <harts-circle>`, 1866).—Given any circular triangle formed by three arbitrary circles, the eight Apollonius circles touching all three partition into groups of four (analogous to the incircle and three excircles of a rectilinear triangle) such that each quartet of four circles is touched by a common circle—Hart’s Circle.
f. 0133 · p. 107

Dem.—Let the three given circles be $A, B, C$. By Prop. 5, invert $A, B, C$ into three circles whose centres are collinear. In this inverted figure, the eight tangent circles reduce to pairs symmetric across the line of centres. Applying Casey’s Theorem to the four tangent circles in each quartet shows that their common tangents satisfy the condition $\overline{12}\cdot\overline{34} + \overline{14}\cdot\overline{23} = \overline{13}\cdot\overline{24}$, proving the existence of a fourth circle tangent to all four. Inverting back, Hart’s Circle touches the four circles of each system!

Prop. 12.—If a triangle be inscribed in a circle and circumscribed about another circle, the inverse of the circumcircle with respect to the incircle is the nine-points circle of the triangle formed by the points of contact.
f. 0134 · p. 108

Cor. 1.—The radius of the nine-points circle is half the radius of the circumcircle.

Cor. 2.—The distance between the circumcentre and incentre satisfies $\delta^2 = R^2 - 2Rr$ (Euler’s formula).

Cor. 3.—The relation $1/(R+\delta) + 1/(R-\delta) = 1/r$ holds identically.

Prop. 13.—If a variable chord of a circle subtend a right angle at a fixed point, the locus of its pole is a circle.
f. 0135 · p. 109

Dem.—Let $O$ be the centre of the circle, $P$ the fixed point, and $AB$ the variable chord subtending a right angle at $P$. Inverting with respect to $P$, the circle becomes a circle, and the chord $AB$ subtends a right angle at $P$; hence the locus of the pole is found by reciprocation to be a circle coaxal with the given circle and point $P$.

Prop. 14.—If a quadrilateral be inscribed in a circle of radius $R$ and circumscribed about a circle of radius $r$, and $\delta$ be the distance between their centres, then

$$ \frac{1}{(R+\delta)^2} + \frac{1}{(R-\delta)^2} = \frac{1}{r^2}. $$

f. 0136 · p. 110

Dem.—By inversion and the property of chords subtending right angles at the contact points (Prop. 13), the bicentric quadrilateral identity follows directly. (Due to Durège; proved by R. F. Davis, Educational Times).

Prop. 15.—Four mutually orthogonal circles can be formed from any orthocentric quadrangle.
f. 0137 · p. 111

Dem.—Let $A, B, C$ be three points, and $H$ their orthocentre. Circles described with centres $A, B, C, H$ and radii equal to the square roots of their respective powers form a mutually orthogonal system.

Prop. 16.—If four circles be mutually orthogonal, and any point or figure be inverted successively with respect to each of the four circles, the fourth inversion coincides with the original figure.
f. 0138 · p. 112

Dem.—Let the four orthogonal circles have centres $A, B, C, D$. Inverting from an intersection of two of them gives two rectangular diameters and an imaginary concentric circle. Successive inversions with respect to these circles turn point $P$ into $Q$ ($OP = -OQ$), and successive reflexions in the two diameters bring $Q$ back to $P$.

Note on Elliptic Functions: The foregoing theorem is important in the Theory of Elliptic Functions, as on it depends the reduction of the rectification of Bicircular and Sphero-Quartics to Elliptic Integrals (see Phil. Trans., vol. 167, Part ii., “On a New Form of Tangential Equation”).

Extension: If five spheres be mutually orthogonal, and if any surface be inverted with respect to each of the five spheres in succession, the fifth inversion will coincide with the original surface.
