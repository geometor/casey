Book VI, Section VI Notes: Desargues, Pascal & the Projective Revolution¶

Historical Provenance & Projective Foundations

f. 0152 · p. 126

1. The Emergence of Projective Geometry¶

Section VI encapsulates the grand seventeenth-century dawn and nineteenth-century resurgence of projective geometry:

  • Girard Desargues (1639): In Brouillon project d’une atteinte aux événemens des rencontres du cône avec un plan, Desargues introduced involution, points at infinity, and the homology of triangles (Desargues’s Theorem, Prop. 5 Cor. 2), creating projective geometry ahead of his time.

  • Blaise Pascal (1640): At the age of sixteen, Pascal published Essay pour les coniques, announcing his celebrated theorem of the mystic hexagram (Pascal’s Theorem, Prop. 4). Casey’s proof using anharmonic cyclic pencils follows the nineteenth-century methods of Steiner and Chasles, demonstrating that sixty Pascal lines arise from any six concyclic points.

  • Michel Chasles (1837, 1865): In his Aperçu historique (1837) and Traité des sections coniques (1865), Chasles established the term rapport anharmonique (“anharmonic ratio”) and the notation $(ABCD)$, proving the projective invariance of the cross-ratio under central and conical projections.

  • Richard Townsend (1863): Senior Fellow of Trinity College Dublin and Casey’s colleague, Townsend published Chapters on the Modern Geometry of the Point, Line, and Circle, from which Casey drew much of the systematic exposition of double points and homographic ranges.

2. Classical Problems & The Butterfly Theorem¶

  • Pappus of Alexandria (c. 320 AD): Synagoge (Collection), Book VII, contains the earliest known lemmas on cross-ratios and what is now known as Pappus’s Hexagon Theorem (Prop. 4 Cor. 3).

  • The Butterfly Theorem (Prop. 2 Cor. 5): First appearing in William Wallace’s The Gentlemen’s Mathematical Companion (1803), this famous theorem is shown by Casey to be a trivial consequence of cyclic anharmonic pencils!

  • Castillon’s Problem (Prop. 11.2): Posed by Gabriel Cramer in 1742 and solved by Jean de Castillon in 1776 (Nouveaux Mémoires de l’Académie de Berlin), the problem of inscribing in a given circle or polygon a polygon whose sides pass through given points is solved projectively through double points of homography in three lines.
