People¶

33 Ancient, Early Modern & Nineteenth-Century Geometers in Casey’s Sequel

Every :person: reference across the 3-Track Book Codex resolves to its biographical anchor below.

Authors, Translators & Editors¶

John Casey (1820–1891)¶

  • Role: Irish Geometer, Professor of Higher Mathematics at the Catholic University of Ireland, Fellow of the Royal University of Ireland, F.R.S.

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Born May 12, 1820, in Kilbehenny, County Limerick; died January 3, 1891, in Dublin. Casey stands alongside George Salmon, Richard Townsend, and Robert Stawell Ball at the summit of the nineteenth-century Dublin school of pure geometry. Educated first in local Munster schools and largely self-taught in higher mathematics while serving as headmaster of the Kilkenny Central Model Schools, Casey entered Trinity College Dublin as a pensioner in 1858 at the age of thirty-eight—having already communicated original discoveries on the geometry of the triangle and circle to Richard Townsend and George Salmon. Electing Scholarship in 1861 and graduating B.A. in 1862, he subsequently served as Professor of Higher Mathematics and Mathematical Physics at the Catholic University of Ireland (from 1873) and Fellow of the Royal University of Ireland (from 1882), elected Fellow of the Royal Society in 1875.

    In 1861, as an undergraduate (Quarterly Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics 4, pp. 245–252), Casey discovered his celebrated three-line circular-inversion proof of Feuerbach’s Theorem—inverting the incircle and the $A$-excircle into themselves from the midpoint $M_a$ of $BC$ with squared radius $\frac{1}{4}(b - c)^2$ so that the fourth common tangent maps directly onto the nine-points circle. In 1866 (Proceedings of the Royal Irish Academy, vol. 9, pp. 396–423), he published his famous generalization of Ptolemy’s Theorem for four circles tangent to a fifth. His Sequel to Euclid (1st ed. 1881; 4th ed. 1886 adding the Supplementary Chapter; 5th ed. 1888; 6th ed. 1892) introduced generations of English-speaking students worldwide to inversion, coaxal circles, poles and polars, and Lemoine–Brocard modern triangle geometry.

Patrick A. E. Dowling (fl. 1888–1892)¶

  • Role: Irish Mathematician, Pupil & Son-in-Law of John Casey, Editor of the 6th Edition of Casey’s Sequel

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Mathematical collaborator and son-in-law of John Casey who posthumously saw through the press and revised the Sixth Edition of A Sequel to the First Six Books of the Elements of Euclid (Dublin: Hodges, Figgis, & Co.; London: Longmans, Green, & Co., 1892; pp. i–xviii, 1–248). Dowling scrupulously preserved the exact proposition and section numbering of Casey’s 1888 Fifth Edition while adding supplementary examples and errata corrections. His 1892 text stands as the canonical witness cited as [casey:sequel:1892] in Nathan Altshiller-Court’s College Geometry.

Euclid of Alexandria (fl. c. 300 BC)¶

  • Role: Author of the Elements (Stoicheia)

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Author of the Stoicheia, whose first six planar books serve as the direct structural foundation for Casey’s Sequel. Casey arranges his modern developments book-by-book alongside the classical Euclidean canon.

Cited Geometers, Philosophers & Scholars¶

Nathan Altshiller-Court (1881–1968)¶

  • Role: Mathematician & Author of College Geometry

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Polish-born University of Oklahoma geometer whose College Geometry (1925; 2nd ed. 1952) cites Casey’s Sequel to Euclid as a foundational English treatise on modern synthetic circle and triangle geometry.

Apollonius of Perga (c. 240 – c. 190 BC)¶

  • Role: Ancient Greek Geometer (‘The Great Geometer’)

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Author of the Conics, De Locis Planis, and De Tactionibus. Casey develops Apollonius’s median theorem (Book II, Prop. 1), the Circle of Apollonius as a coaxal constant-ratio locus (Book VI, Sec. V), and the Problem of Apollonius via centres of similitude and Gergonne’s pole-polar method.

Charles Julien Brianchon (1783–1864)¶

  • Role: French Projective Geometer & Co-Discoverer of the Nine-Points Circle

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Student at the École Polytechnique who derived Brianchon’s Theorem on circumscribed hexagons by polar reciprocation of Pascal’s Hexagrammum Mysticum (1806) and co-published the nine-points circle theorem with Poncelet (1820–1821).

Henri Brocard (1845–1922)¶

  • Role: French Meteorological Officer & Co-Founder of Recent Triangle Geometry

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Discovered the properties of the Brocard points (Ω, Ω’), the Brocard angle ω, Brocard’s First and Second Triangles, and the seven-point Brocard Circle (AFAS Alger, 1881), expounded systematically in Casey’s Supplementary Chapter (Secs. II & V).

Giovanni Ceva (1647–1734)¶

  • Role: Italian Geometer & Hydraulic Engineer

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Published De lineis rectis se invicem secantibus statica constructio (1678), establishing the concurrency ratio criterion for three cevians presented in Casey Book VI, Sec. I, Prop. 2 (p. 67).

Michel Chasles (1793–1880)¶

  • Role: French Projective Geometer & Historian of Geometry

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Systematized signed segments, anharmonic (cross) ratios, and homographic ranges and pencils in the Aperçu historique (1837) and Traité de géométrie supérieure (1852), forming the backbone of Casey Book VI, Sec. VI.

Girard Desargues (1591–1661)¶

  • Role: French Architect & Founder of Projective Geometry

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Established perspective homology of triangles, involution on secants, and harmonic pole-polar properties. Casey proves Desargues’s Theorem both by Menelaus/anharmonic ratios (Book VI, Sec. VI) and by polar reciprocation (Sec. VII, p. 142).

Leonhard Euler (1707–1783)¶

  • Role: Swiss Mathematician & Physicist

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Proved the collinearity and 2:1 trisection of the circumcentre O, centroid G, and orthocentre H (1765), the quadrilateral midpoint-diagonal sum-of-squares theorem (Casey Book II, Prop. 2), and the four-point collinearity identity AB·CD + BC·AD = AC·BD (Book II, Prop. 7).

Karl Wilhelm Feuerbach (1800–1834)¶

  • Role: German Geometer

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Published Eigenschaften einiger merkwürdigen Punkte des geradlinigen Dreiecks (1822), proving that the circle through the side midpoints touches the incircle internally and all three excircles externally. Casey presents three distinct synthetic proofs of Feuerbach’s Theorem.

Louis Gaultier (c. 1776–c. 1840)¶

  • Role: French Geometer (École Polytechnique)

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Introduced the term ‘radical axis’ (axe radical) and ‘radical centre’ in the Journal de l’École Polytechnique (1813), foundational to Casey Book III, Props. 23–24.

Joseph Diaz Gergonne (1771–1859)¶

  • Role: French Mathematician & Codifier of Projective Duality

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Formulated the principle of duality, introduced the words ‘pole’ and ‘polar’, discovered the Gergonne concurrency point of incircle contact cevians, and solved Apollonius’s problem via radical centre and poles.

Andrew Searle Hart (1811–1890)¶

  • Role: Irish Geometer & Vice-Provost of Trinity College Dublin

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Mentor and colleague of Casey in Dublin; generalized Feuerbach’s Theorem from rectilinear triangles to circular triangles formed by three arbitrary circles (Hart’s Circle, Quarterly Journal of Mathematics, 1861), proved in Casey Book VI, Sec. IV, Prop. 11 (pp. 106–108).

Philippe de La Hire (1640–1718)¶

  • Role: French Projective Geometer & Astronomer

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Discovered the fundamental reciprocity theorem of poles and polars in Sectiones Conicae (1685): if Q lies on the polar of P, then P lies on the polar of Q (Casey Book III, Prop. 25, p. 45).

Émile Michel Hyacinthe Lemoine (1840–1912)¶

  • Role: French Civil Engineer & Co-Founder of Modern Triangle Geometry

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Revived synthetic triangle geometry with his 1873 AFAS Lyon memoir on the symmedian point K = X(6), the First Lemoine Circle (triplicate-ratio circle), and the Cosine (Second Lemoine) Circle, central to Casey’s Supplementary Chapter.

Gaston Albert Gohierre de Longchamps (1842–1906)¶

  • Role: French Mathematician

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Investigated the point X(20) (reflection of orthocentre H across circumcentre O) and Longchamps’ Circle orthogonal to the median-radius circles at the vertices/midpoints (Casey Supp. Ch., Misc. Ex. 146–158, p. 247).

William J. M’Clelland (fl. 1885–1900)¶

  • Role: Irish Geometer

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Author of A Treatise on the Geometry of the Circle (1891) and contributor of the second similitude proof of Feuerbach’s Theorem acknowledged by Casey in the Preface and Supplementary Chapter (pp. vi, 201–203).

Menelaus of Alexandria (c. 70 – c. 140 CE)¶

  • Role: Greco-Roman Mathematician & Astronomer

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Formulated in Sphaerica III.1 the transversal ratio theorem for triangles, proved for planar directed ratios in Casey Book VI, Sec. I, Prop. 4 (p. 69).

Gaspard Monge (1746–1818)¶

  • Role: French Geometer & Creator of Descriptive Geometry

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Proved that the six centres of similitude of three circles lie three by three upon four axes of similitude (Casey Book VI, Sec. II).

Joseph Jean Baptiste Neuberg (1840–1926)¶

  • Role: Luxembourgeois-Belgian Geometer (University of Liège)

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Close correspondent of Casey (thanked warmly in Casey’s Preface, p. vi); discovered the Neuberg circles, Neuberg cubic, and associated Brocard–Steiner–Tarry theorems integrated into Casey’s Supplementary Chapter.

Pappus of Alexandria (c. 290 – c. 350 CE)¶

  • Role: Late Antique Greek Geometer (Author of the Synagoge)

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Preserved cross-ratio invariance, complete-quadrilateral harmonic properties, locus to three/four lines, and the ancient Arbelos shoemaker’s-knife chain theorem proved by inversion in Casey Book VI, Sec. IV, Prop. 9 Cor. 1 (p. 103).

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662)¶

  • Role: French Mathematician, Physicist & Philosopher

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Discovered at age sixteen the Hexagrammum Mysticum (Essai pour les coniques, 1640) that opposite sides of a hexagon inscribed in a circle or conic meet collinearly (Casey Book VI, Sec. VI).

Jean-Victor Poncelet (1788–1867)¶

  • Role: French Geometer & Author of the Traité des propriétés projectives des figures

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Developed coaxal pencils of circles and their real/ideal limiting points (points limites), polar reciprocation, and the nine-points circle (1822), deeply shaping Units 2–4 of Casey’s Sequel.

Claudius Ptolemy (c. 100 – c. 170 CE)¶

  • Role: Greco-Egyptian Astronomer & Mathematician

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Proved in Almagest I.10 that in a cyclic quadrilateral AB·CD + AD·BC = AC·BD—the zero-radius specialization of Casey’s 1866 Generalized Ptolemy Theorem (Book VI, Sec. IV, Prop. 10, p. 103).

George Salmon (1819–1904)¶

  • Role: Irish Mathematician & Provost of Trinity College Dublin

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Author of A Treatise on Conic Sections (1848) who encouraged Casey’s geometric career at Trinity College Dublin and proved Salmon’s harmonic-distance theorem for poles and polars (Casey Book VI, Sec. VII, Prop. 3).

Jakob Steiner (1796–1863)¶

  • Role: Swiss Synthetic Geometer (University of Berlin)

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Formulated the power of a point (Potenz eines Punktes, 1826), inversion constructions, complete-quadrangle straightedge polar constructions, and Steiner’s point X(99) on the circumcircle (Casey Supp. Ch., Sec. VII).

Matthew Stewart (1717–1785)¶

  • Role: Scottish Geometer (University of Edinburgh)

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Published Some General Theorems of Considerable Use in the Higher Parts of Mathematics (1746), stating the cevian metric relation proved in Casey Book II, Prop. 10 (p. 26).

Gaston Tarry (1843–1913)¶

  • Role: French Mathematician

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Discovered Tarry’s point X(98) on the circumcircle—antipodal to Steiner’s point X(99) and centre of concurrency of perpendiculars from the vertices of ABC to the sides of Brocard’s First Triangle (Casey Supp. Ch., Sec. VII).

Henry Martyn Taylor (1842–1927)¶

  • Role: English Mathematician (Trinity College, Cambridge)

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Proved that the six orthogonal projections of the three altitude feet upon the other two sides of a triangle lie on a single Tucker circle—Taylor’s Circle (Messenger of Mathematics, 1882; Casey Supp. Ch., Sec. III).

Richard Townsend (1821–1884)¶

  • Role: Irish Geometer & Fellow of Trinity College Dublin

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Author of Chapters on the Modern Geometry of the Point, Line, and Circle (1863–1865) and mentor to John Casey at Trinity College Dublin.

Robert Tucker (1832–1905)¶

  • Role: English Mathematician & Honorary Secretary of the London Mathematical Society

  • Historical & Geometric Context: Discovered the one-parameter family of six-point Tucker Circles generalizing the First Lemoine, Cosine, Taylor, and circumcircle configurations (1883; thanked in Casey’s Preface, p. vi, and expounded in Supp. Ch., Sec. III).
